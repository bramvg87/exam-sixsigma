SECTION 01

The frame: innocent until proven guilty

Hypothesis testing is a decision rule under uncertainty, not a proof. Every test you run reduces to "is my data unusual enough that I can't keep believing the boring story?"

Every test has two competing stories. The null hypothesis (written H₀) is the boring default you assume is true until the data forces you to abandon it. The alternative hypothesis (H₁) is what you actually suspect.

You only reject H₀ if the data would be too unusual to have happened by chance under H₀. Otherwise you stick with H₀, the way a court returns "not guilty" rather than "proven innocent."

Because you're making a yes/no call from a noisy sample, you can make two kinds of mistake. The risks you accept for each one are called α (alpha) and β (beta).

The two risks. α is your tolerance for false alarms (typically 5%). β is your tolerance for misses (typically 20%). Both are choices you make before running the test. Together they let you make calibrated calls from imperfect data.
Think of the smoke alarm in your kitchen. α is how often it shrieks when nothing's burning. β is how often it stays silent when there really is a fire. You want both low, but they trade off, and the only way to reduce both at once is to listen to more "signal" (a bigger sample, or a better sensor).
SECTION 02

The example: a jar-filling line

One scenario carried through the whole tutorial. Change the data and watch every other number follow.

A jar-filling line is meant to deliver 250 g net per jar. Maintenance adjusted a valve last week and you want to verify the line is still on target. You pull 10 jars at random and weigh them.

H₀: μ = 250 g (the line is on target)  ·  H₁: μ ≠ 250 g (it has drifted)

Two-sided because you care about underfill (legal, customer complaint) and overfill (giveaway cost) equally. Here are your ten measurements:

Two quantities matter here, and one of them is sneaky:

x̄ is the sample mean, the average of your 10 jars. It's an estimate of the true population mean μ that you'll never directly know.

s is the sample standard deviation, your estimate of how much the process naturally wobbles. Crucially, s is not σ. σ (sigma) is the true population standard deviation, an unknown property of the process itself. s is what you compute from your sample to estimate σ. The whole reason the t-test exists is this gap between the true σ you'd love to know and the messy s you actually have.

SECTION 03

The four numbers you choose: α, β, δ, σ

Three are judgment calls, one comes from your historical data, and a fifth (n, the sample size) falls out of the formula that ties them together.

α (alpha) is the false-alarm rate you'll accept. Convention: 0.05. You're saying "if H₀ is really true, I'll mistakenly flag the line at most 5% of the time."

β (beta) is the miss rate you'll accept. Convention: 0.20, which makes the test's power equal to 0.80. You're saying "if H₀ is really false by an amount I care about, I want at least an 80% chance of catching it."

δ (delta) is the smallest effect you'd actually care about. This is a business call, not a statistical one. For our jar line, you might pick 2 g: a drift smaller than that is below the noise of normal operation and not worth chasing.

σ (sigma) is the natural noise of the process, taken from historical SPC data or a small pilot. For our line, σ ≈ 2 g based on past control charts.

Once you fix those four, the required sample size is determined by:

n ≈ ( (zα/2 + zβ) · σ / δ )²
zα/2 and zβ are standard-normal critical values for your chosen α and β.

Five levers, one equation. Once you've fixed any four of {α, β, δ, σ, n}, the fifth is determined. Three useful framings:

"How big a sample do I need?" Fix α, β, δ, σ, solve for n.

"I only have n = 10, what shift can I reliably detect?" Fix α, β, σ, n, solve for δ.

"The study said no significant difference, was it big enough?" Fix α, δ, σ, n, solve for β. If power was 30%, "no significant difference" doesn't mean "no difference," it means the test was too small to see one.

SECTION 04

Why a t-test, and what t actually means

The t-distribution exists because you don't know σ and have to estimate it from your sample. That extra uncertainty earns you fatter tails.

If you knew the true population σ, you could use a z-test with the standard normal distribution. You don't, so you use s as your estimate of σ. That's a second source of uncertainty on top of the sample mean, and the math accounts for it by switching from the normal distribution to the t-distribution, which has fatter tails (more weight in the extremes).

The t-distribution has one parameter, the degrees of freedom, equal to n − 1. Small n means very fat tails (a lot of uncertainty from estimating σ with a tiny sample). As n grows, the t-distribution converges back to the normal distribution and the difference disappears.

Standard normal (z) versus t with df = 9
Same area under each curve; t puts more of it in the tails to account for estimating σ.

What t actually means. The t-statistic is a signal-to-noise ratio:

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)
Signal divided by noise: how far is your observation from H₀, in units of typical sampling wobble?

The numerator is the signal: the gap between what you observed (x̄) and what H₀ claims (μ₀). The denominator is the noise: the standard error, which is the typical amount that sample means wobble around the true mean just from random sampling. So t = −2.71 reads as "the observed gap is 2.71 times bigger than the typical random wobble." Big enough that random sampling alone struggles to explain it.

SECTION 05

Running the test end-to-end

All the pieces in place. Plug in the numbers, get a t, get a p, make a decision.

From section 2, your data gave x̄ = 248.2, s = 2.15, n = 10. The test statistic is:

t = (248.2 − 250) / (2.15 / √10) = −1.8 / 0.680 ≈ −2.65

With df = 9, looking up the two-sided probability of seeing |t| ≥ 2.65 by chance gives p ≈ 0.026. Compare to α = 0.05: p < α, so reject H₀.

How to interpret it. There is statistically significant evidence at α = 0.05 that the average fill weight is no longer 250 g; the sample points to a small underfill near 248 g. The probability of seeing a deviation this large purely by chance, if the line were really on target, is only about 2.6%, low enough to act on. Investigate the recent valve adjustment.

What you did not prove. You did not prove the line is "broken." You did not prove the true mean is exactly 248.2. You did not exclude the possibility that you got unlucky (that 2.6% chance is real). A hypothesis test is a decision rule under uncertainty, not a proof.

SECTION 06

The proportion cousin: π, P, and E(P)

Same framework, applied to defect rates instead of fill weights. Different test statistic, identical logic.

So far we've tested a mean (the average fill weight). But Six Sigma is often about proportions: what fraction of jars have a bad seal, what percentage of welds fail inspection, what share of shipments arrive damaged. Three symbols matter:

π (pi) is the true proportion of defects in the whole population, an unknown property of the process. If your line genuinely produces 2% bad seals, then π = 0.02. You can almost never measure π directly because you can't inspect every jar ever made.

P (sometimes written p̂, "p-hat") is the sample proportion you actually observed. Inspect 200 jars, find 6 bad ones, and P = 6 / 200 = 0.03. P changes every time you take a new sample. It's noisy.

E(P) is the expected value of P, meaning the long-run average of P if you repeated the sampling endlessly. For an unbiased process, E(P) = π. This is the bridge between what you measure and what is true: your noisy sample proportion P bounces around, but on average it lands exactly on π. Calling E(P) "the expected percentage of errors" is a fine way to remember it, because that's exactly what it is.

expected number of defects = n · π  ·  standard error of P = √(π(1−π) / n)

The hypothesis test for proportions. Same machinery as before. Say the line is supposed to run at 2% defects (π₀ = 0.02). You inspect 200 jars and find 8 bad ones (P = 0.04). Is the rate really higher, or could 8/200 happen by chance even when the true rate is still 2%?

H₀: π = 0.02  ·  H₁: π > 0.02
test statistic: z = (P − π₀) / √(π₀(1−π₀)/n)

Plugging in: z = (0.04 − 0.02) / √(0.02 · 0.98 / 200) = 0.02 / 0.0099 ≈ 2.02. For a one-sided test, p ≈ 0.022, so reject H₀ at α = 0.05. The defect rate has genuinely crept up.

Everything you learned about α, β, sample size, and decision rules transfers. Only the test statistic changes (z based on a proportion's standard error, instead of t based on a mean's standard error).

SECTION 07

What to remember

The big ideas, the formulas worth keeping, and the traps to watch for.

The framework

H₀ is the boring default. H₁ is what you suspect.

Compute a test statistic. If the data would be too rare under H₀, reject H₀. Otherwise stick with it.

"Not significant" means "not enough evidence to reject H₀", not "H₀ is true."

The two risks

α = false-alarm rate (Type I). Conventionally 0.05.

β = miss rate (Type II). Conventionally 0.20. Power = 1 − β.

Both are choices, fixed before you see the data.

The t-test, in one breath

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)

Use t (not z) because σ is unknown and estimated as s. Compare against t-distribution with df = n − 1.

t reads as signal / noise: observed gap divided by typical sampling wobble.

Sample size design

n ≈ ((zα/2 + zβ) · σ / δ)²

Five levers (α, β, δ, σ, n), one equation. Fix any four, the fifth follows.

σ comes from historical data, never invented.

Decision rule

Reject H₀ if p < α.

Equivalent: reject if |t| > tcritical.

p is the area in the t-distribution's tails beyond your observed |t|. It tells you how unusual your data would be under H₀.

Proportions

π = true population defect rate.

P = sample proportion observed.

E(P) = π: the expected percentage of errors equals the true rate. The bridge from sample to population.

Traps to avoid

Failing to reject ≠ proving H₀. Always check the power.

p is not the probability that H₀ is true. It's the probability of data this extreme assuming H₀ is true.

Statistical significance ≠ practical importance. A 0.01 g shift on a 250 g jar can be highly significant with n = 100000, and still operationally irrelevant.

In one sentence

A hypothesis test asks "is the gap between what I see and what H₀ claims bigger than the random wobble I'd expect under H₀?" The t-test is just the version that uses s instead of σ to measure the wobble.
